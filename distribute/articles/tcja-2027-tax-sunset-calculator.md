TCJA provisions sunset December 31, 2026. Here's what your 2027 taxes could look like — and a free calculator that shows you exactly how much more (or less) you could owe.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 was never meant to last forever. Most of its individual-side provisions were written with an expiration date: midnight on December 31, 2026. Unless Congress acts, on January 1, 2027 the tax code reverts to a pre-TCJA version — adjusted for inflation, but structurally different from what W-2 earners and small-business owners have been filing for the last eight years.

The [One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1), signed into law in July 2025, made *some* TCJA provisions permanent — notably parts of the estate tax regime and the 20% pass-through deduction with modifications. But it did **not** extend the individual income tax brackets in their current form, and several planning-critical provisions are still set to expire. If you're doing any 2026 tax planning, this matters.

## What actually changes on January 1, 2027

Here's the short list of changes that will hit most filers:

- **Individual tax brackets revert.** Today's bracket structure — 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, 37% — snaps back to the pre-TCJA structure: 10%, 15%, 25%, 28%, 33%, 35%, 39.6%. The top rate rises. More importantly, the middle brackets widen at higher rates, so middle-income households get hit first.
- **Standard deduction roughly halves.** For 2026, the standard deduction is projected around $30,000 MFJ / $15,000 single. In 2027 it reverts to roughly $16,500 MFJ / $8,250 single (inflation-adjusted from the pre-TCJA base).
- **Personal exemptions return.** Eliminated under TCJA, these come back at around $5,300 per person (inflation-adjusted) — partially offsetting the smaller standard deduction for larger households.
- **Child Tax Credit drops.** The $2,000 CTC reverts to $1,000 per child with tighter phase-outs.
- **SALT cap changes.** The $10,000 cap on state and local tax deductions expires, so itemizers in high-tax states (CA, NY, NJ, IL) can once again deduct the full amount. This is one of the few changes that benefits higher-income filers.
- **Mortgage interest.** The deduction cap returns to $1M of acquisition debt (up from $750K), and home equity interest becomes deductible again within limits.
- **Estate exemption.** Scheduled to halve from roughly $14M to about $7M per person before OBBBA — the 2025 law kept this one higher, but planning windows still matter.
- **QBI (Section 199A) pass-through deduction.** The 20% deduction for sole proprietors, S-corps, and partnerships was extended by OBBBA but with new caps and phase-outs — small-business owners need to re-run their numbers.
- **AMT.** The alternative minimum tax exemption shrinks, pulling more upper-middle-income households back into AMT territory.

## A worked example: $120K married couple

Consider a married couple filing jointly earning $120,000 in wages, taking the standard deduction, with two kids.

**2026 (current law):**
- Taxable income after standard deduction: ~$90,000
- Federal tax under TCJA brackets: ~$11,200
- Child Tax Credit: -$4,000
- **Net federal tax: ~$7,200**

**2027 (post-sunset):**
- Taxable income after smaller standard deduction + 4 personal exemptions (~$21,200): ~$82,300
- Federal tax under pre-TCJA brackets: ~$12,400
- Child Tax Credit: -$2,000
- **Net federal tax: ~$10,400**

Difference: about **$3,200 more owed** in 2027 on the same income. The Tax Foundation's [TCJA expiration analysis](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/2026-tax-brackets/) shows similar results across the middle quintiles — most households in the $75K–$200K range owe $2,000–$5,000 more after the sunset, depending on family size and state.

Higher earners in high-tax states may actually come out slightly ahead because of SALT cap removal. Lower earners with multiple dependents benefit from the return of personal exemptions. Everyone else pays more.

## The planning window that closes December 31, 2026

If 2027 taxes are likely higher for you, the year 2026 becomes a planning opportunity:

- **Roth conversions** — converting traditional IRA dollars at today's lower rates, paying tax in the 22% or 24% bracket instead of tomorrow's 25% or 28%.
- **Income acceleration** — if you control timing (bonuses, consulting income, stock sales), pulling income into 2026 can save real money.
- **Deduction deferral** — pushing deductible expenses into 2027 may be worth more when marginal rates are higher (and SALT cap is gone).
- **Gifting and estate planning** — the current exemption remains generous under OBBBA, but state-level estate taxes and future legislation create their own timing questions.
- **Small-business structure review** — QBI rules changed under OBBBA. S-corp vs. sole-prop math may have flipped for your situation.

The Joint Committee on Taxation's [scoring of the expiring provisions](https://www.jct.gov/publications/2024/) and Grant Thornton's [2026 Tax Planning Guide](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/tax-planning-guide) both walk through these strategies in detail. Worth a read before year-end.
