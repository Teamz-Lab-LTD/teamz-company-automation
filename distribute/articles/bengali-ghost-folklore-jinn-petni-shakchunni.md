Most English-language horror borrows from the same short list: vampires, poltergeists, possessed houses. Bengali folklore runs on a completely different cast, and almost none of it is written down in English. Here is what the villages of rural Bangladesh actually talk about after dark.

## Jinn

Not a ghost. In Islamic belief a jinn is a created being with free will, living alongside humans rather than after them — which is why village stories treat jinn as neighbours to be respected rather than spirits to be banished. They are said to prefer abandoned places, old trees, riverbanks and crossroads. The fear is less "it will kill you" and more "you have wandered into somewhere that already belongs to something else."

This distinction matters and is usually lost in translation. Calling a jinn a "ghost" flattens the belief entirely.

## Petni and Shakchunni

Both are female spirits, and both come from the same idea: a woman who died with something unfinished.

**Shakchunni** is specifically the spirit of a married woman who died before living out her married life. She is recognised by the *shakha* — the white conch-shell bangles a married Bengali woman wears. The classic story has her displacing a living wife and taking her place in the household, which nobody notices for a long time.

**Petni** is the broader term, often a woman who died unmarried or by drowning. She is associated with tamarind trees, which is why village elders tell children not to sleep under one.

Read as folklore, both are stories about how a society treated women whose lives did not follow the expected path. The horror carries a social argument inside it.

## Mechho Bhoot

The fish-eating ghost. Haunts ponds and riverbanks, drawn by the smell of fish, and is blamed when a catch goes missing. Almost comic compared to the others — and that is the point. Not every Bengali spirit is a monster. Some are nuisances.

## Nishi Daak — the night call

The one that genuinely unsettles people who grew up with it.

The nishi is said to call your name in the voice of someone you love — a mother, a spouse, a child — from outside the house at night. If you answer the first call, nothing happens. Answer the second and you follow the voice out and do not come back.

The rule every Bengali child learns: **never answer a voice calling your name at night until it has called twice.**

Unlike a haunted house you can leave, the nishi uses the thing you trust most — a familiar voice — against you. That is a genuinely modern kind of fear, and it is centuries old.

## Why these stories survive

Rural Bangladesh has electricity, phones and satellite TV, and the stories have not gone anywhere. Partly that is because they are tied to real places — a specific tamarind tree, a specific pond, a specific bend in the road — so the landscape keeps retelling them. Partly it is because they encode practical warnings: do not walk alone at night, do not swim in that pond, do not go near the abandoned house.

Every culture wraps its safety rules in a monster. Bengal just has better monsters.
