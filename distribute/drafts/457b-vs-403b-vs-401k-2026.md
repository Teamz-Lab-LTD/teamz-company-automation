# 457(b) vs 403(b) vs 401(k) — Which Public Sector Retirement Plan Wins in 2026

If you work for a public school, state government, or non-profit hospital, you may have access to retirement plans most private-sector workers don't know exist. The most powerful: the **457(b)**, which has **no 10% early-withdrawal penalty** and lets you double-dip with a 403(b) for combined $47,000+ annual deferral.

## The 2026 Contribution Limits

All three plans share the same employee deferral limit for 2026:
- **$23,500** standard limit
- **+$7,500** age 50+ catch-up
- **+$11,250** special age 60-63 catch-up (instead of $7,500, new under SECURE 2.0)

But the 403(b) AND 457(b) limits are **separate** — meaning teachers, university faculty, and public hospital employees with access to both can max each independently for combined $47,000 annual deferral, or $58,750 at age 60-63.

## The 457(b) Double-Dip Strategy

This is the most underused tax shelter for public-sector workers. If your employer offers both a 403(b) and a 457(b) — common in school districts and public universities — you can contribute $23,500 to each in 2026:

- 403(b): $23,500 in pre-tax (or Roth) contributions
- 457(b): $23,500 separate
- **Combined: $47,000** of tax-deferred space

At age 50+ with both catch-ups: $62,000. At age 60-63 with the new special catch-up: $69,500 combined.

The IRS treats them as separate because they're governed by different code sections — 403(b) under §402(g), 457(b) under §457(e)(18).

## The No-Penalty Withdrawal Trick

Governmental 457(b) plans have **no 10% early-withdrawal penalty**, regardless of age. Once you separate from service (retire, quit, leave the role), you can access 457(b) funds at any age subject only to ordinary income tax. No "Rule of 55," no 72(t) gymnastics.

This makes 457(b) the perfect "bridge fund" for early retirees:
- Retire at 55, draw down 457(b) penalty-free
- Let 403(b) and IRA grow untouched until 59½
- Maximize tax efficiency by sequencing withdrawals

## Important Catch — Non-Governmental 457(b)

Non-governmental 457(b) plans (some 501(c)(3) employers) have different rules:
- Assets remain employer's until distributed (creditor risk in bankruptcy)
- Limited early withdrawal options
- Cannot roll over to IRA

Always verify if your 457(b) is **governmental** before relying on it. School districts, state governments, and county hospitals = governmental. Some non-profits = non-governmental.
