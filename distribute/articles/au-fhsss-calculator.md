The First Home Super Saver Scheme lets first home buyers save their deposit inside superannuation, where earnings are taxed at 15% instead of their marginal rate. When they're ready to buy, they withdraw up to $50,000 and pay tax at their marginal rate minus a 30% tax offset. For anyone on 32.5% or higher, the tax saving is real and significant.

## How Much Can You Actually Save Through FHSS?

The annual contribution limit is $15,000 per financial year, and the total withdrawal limit is $50,000. Both voluntary concessional (pre-tax salary sacrifice) and non-concessional (after-tax) contributions count toward the limit.

**Example: Income $85,000/year, contributing $15,000/year for 3 years via salary sacrifice**
- Without FHSS: $15,000 after 32.5% tax + 2% Medicare = $9,975 saved per year
- With FHSS: $15,000 at 15% contributions tax = $12,750 goes in; earnings at 15% inside super
- Tax saving on contributions alone: ~$2,625/year = ~$7,875 over 3 years
- Plus earnings inside super (deeming rate applied on withdrawal, but still advantageous)

The actual withdrawal amount is the contributions plus an ATO-calculated earnings amount using the shortfall interest charge rate (~4.77% in 2025-26). This isn't your actual fund earnings — it's a deemed rate applied to your FHSS contributions.

## The Catch: Timing and Flexibility

Once you apply to the ATO to release FHSS amounts, you must sign a contract to purchase a property within 12 months (extendable to 24 months). If you don't buy, the withdrawn amount must go back into super or you face a 20% FHSS tax.

You can't withdraw specific contributions — the ATO determines the maximum releasable amount. And contributions already used for FHSS can't be counted again later.

## Who Benefits Most?

FHSS makes most sense for people on:
- 32.5% marginal rate or above ($45,001–$120,000 income)
- A stable employment situation allowing salary sacrifice
- A 2–4 year saving horizon

Those on 19% marginal rate (under $45,000) get a smaller benefit because the tax saving is narrower.
