Employment Insurance (EI) max weekly benefit just increased to $695 in January 2026 — up from $688 in 2025. The clawback threshold also moved to $79,000. Most Canadian EI calculators online still show 2024 numbers ($668 max). Here is the actual 2026 math, plus a free calculator that uses current rates.

## What Changed in 2026

Effective January 1, 2026:

- **Maximum Insurable Earnings (MIE):** $65,700 (up from $63,200 in 2025)
- **Maximum weekly benefit:** $695 (up from $688)
- **Annual MIE-based contribution cap:** $1,090.62
- **Employee EI premium rate:** 1.66% (Quebec: 1.31% due to QPIP)
- **Clawback threshold:** $79,000 net income (up from $76,875)

The 55% replacement rate is unchanged. Working While on Claim provisions extended through 2027.

## How EI Actually Works

EI regular benefits replace 55% of your average best weekly insurable earnings, capped at the $695 weekly maximum. The number of "best weeks" used in the average depends on your regional unemployment rate:

- 6% or less unemployment → best 14 weeks
- 6 to 8% → best 16 weeks
- 8% or more → best 20 to 22 weeks

Higher regional unemployment also means longer benefit duration: 14 to 45 weeks of benefits depending on insurable hours and the regional rate.

Insurable hours requirement: 420 (high unemployment region) to 700 (low unemployment region) hours during the 52-week qualifying period.

There is a one-week unpaid waiting period before benefits start. File your claim immediately on your last day of work — Service Canada cannot pay benefits for any week before your filing date.

## The 2026 Clawback — Whom It Affects

If your annual net income exceeds $79,000 in 2026, you must repay 30% of the lesser of (a) your net income above $79,000 or (b) total regular EI benefits received.

Example: laid off in March, found new job in August at $90,000 salary. Total 2026 income: $89,000 (job + EI). EI received: $10,000. Excess over $79,000: $10,000. Clawback = 30% × min($10,000, $10,000) = $3,000 owed at tax time April 30, 2027.

First-time EI claimants and special benefit recipients (maternity, parental, sickness) are exempt.

## Working Part-Time While On EI

The Working While on Claim provision lets you keep up to 25% of your weekly benefit (or $50, whichever is higher) without deduction. Earnings above that threshold are deducted dollar-for-dollar.

Example: $500 weekly EI benefit. Earn up to $125 (25%) without reduction. Earn $300 — first $125 exempt, $175 deducted from EI. You receive $325 EI + $300 wages = $625 total. Always net positive to work.

This provision encourages claimants to take part-time or casual work while job-searching.

## Filing Tips

- File the day you lose your job. Even a one-week delay can cost you a week of benefits.
- Get your Record of Employment (ROE) from your employer — Service Canada needs it.
- First payment typically arrives 28 to 35 days after filing if documents are complete.
- Expected annual income matters — Service Canada deducts tax at source based on it.

For Quebec residents: regular EI works alongside QPIP (Quebec Parental Insurance Plan) for parental benefits. Use the [Quebec Income Tax Calculator](https://tool.teamzlab.com/ca/quebec-income-tax-calculator/) to model the combined impact.
